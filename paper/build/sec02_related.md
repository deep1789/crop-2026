# Related work

**Machine learning for crop yield.** Systematic reviews describe a literature dominated by tree ensembles, neural networks and regularised regression applied to climate, soil, management and remote-sensing covariates [@klompenburg2020]. Evaluation designs in this literature vary, and a recent critique of common issues in published crop-yield studies points to validation that does not match the intended use, including random cross-validation of spatially and temporally structured data [@filippi2024]. A recent preprint reports that cross-country transfer of yield models in Sub-Saharan Africa is far worse than within-country skill suggests [@adjei2026]. Our contribution is complementary: we dissect one concrete, publicly shared benchmark file, quantify the effect of its construction on evaluation, and extend the question from point accuracy to interval coverage.

**Leakage, duplicates and structured validation.** Leakage has been catalogued across disciplines and is a major source of irreproducible results [@kapoor2023]. Benchmark duplicates are a specific form: near-duplicate images shared by training and test sets of CIFAR inflate reported accuracy [@barz2020], and general guidance on avoiding machine-learning pitfalls lists such overlap among the most common errors [@lones2021]. For data with spatial, temporal, hierarchical or phylogenetic structure, blocked cross-validation gives more realistic estimates than random folds [@roberts2017], and spatial validation has overturned apparent skill in large-scale ecological mapping [@ploton2020]. The WILDS benchmarks make the same point for domain shift in general [@koh2021]. Mixed-effects approaches show that standard tree ensembles ignore within-cluster dependence [@hajjem2014]. We build on these results by tracing the origin of duplication in a specific file, by relating the memorisation of group identity to the behaviour of conformal intervals, and by comparing model families on the same data.

**Conformal prediction under shift.** Split conformal prediction gives finite-sample marginal coverage for exchangeable data [@vovk2005; @lei2018; @angelopoulos2023]. When exchangeability fails, coverage can degrade; weighted conformal prediction restores validity under a known covariate shift [@tibshirani2019], and non-exchangeable variants bound the coverage loss [@barber2023]. Cross-conformal and jackknife+ methods reuse all data for calibration [@barber2021]. Locally adaptive intervals use a model of the residual scale [@romano2019], and Mondrian or group-conditional calibration targets coverage within groups [@vovk2013; @gibbs2025]. Prediction sets for two-layer hierarchical data treat groups as the exchangeable unit [@dunn2023]. In agriculture, conformal prediction has been analysed for image-based decision support [@farag2025], and group-conditional calibration has been used for crop and weed classification [@melki2023]. We are not aware of work on tabular yield regression that examines how calibration on random rows fails for unseen countries or districts. Our contribution is empirical and diagnostic: we show when naive calibration collapses, relate the collapse to the inflation ratio of group leakage, and compare these existing remedies (group-aware, locally scaled, weighted and cross-conformal) under a common protocol.

**Spatial and group-weighted conformal methods.** Conformal methods for spatial data localise the calibration, using localized quantile regression [@jiang2024] or geographically weighted scores [@lou2025], and group-weighted conformal prediction treats the group as the determinant of covariate shift between training and test populations [@bhattacharyya2026]. These methods rely on overlap between the calibration and test populations. Here the test groups are new, calibration and test sets are perfectly separable by a classifier, and we show in Section 6.5 why weighting then has nothing to work with.

**Informal notes on the FAO-derived file.** Public code repositories built on the file record that it has 13,130 unique keys among 28,242 rows, that the repeated rows differ only in temperature, and that random splits give optimistic scores [@pnastra; @sivarjun]. These notes are not peer-reviewed and do not trace the join mechanism row by row, quantify the effect on protocols, or study intervals; we credit them and build on them.

**Applicability of spatial models.** The area-of-applicability approach flags prediction locations whose predictor values lie outside the range seen in training [@meyer2021]. We test a related group-level distance score and a learned residual-scale score as indicators of where intervals are likely to be too narrow.
